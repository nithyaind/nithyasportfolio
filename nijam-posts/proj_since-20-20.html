Multimedia Story

SINCE 20/20

WhoAmanda When2026 Hearst National Journalism Awards, June 2026 WhereSan Francisco, CA

Why

What is the future of storytelling and journalism in a world of technology?

What

A multimedia story exploring the intersection of journalism, technology, social impact, and Amanda's story.

How

On May 30th, I landed in San Francisco as one of five finalists for the Hearst Journalism Awards — the national championship of college journalism, where finalists are dropped into a city with a camera and three and a half days to find and tell a story from scratch. No assignment. No sources. Just a theme: survival. Sunday morning, 5:30am. I planted myself in Chinatown and spent seven hours talking to anyone who would have me. Rose, 68, former Miss Chinatown, who had just reunited with her teenage love after fifty years. The Chinatown patrol, who have quietly protected their community from police brutality since the pandemic with no recognition and no fanfare. A martial arts actor who worked with Jackie Chan. An architect sketching in a notebook who told me, casually, that he hoped to one day build in outer space. Then I met Amanda. Amanda is 48. She lost her vision in 2020. When I found her, she was navigating the Chinatown parade alone. Before I ever raised my camera, she had already taken a photo of me with her Meta glasses. Over coffee, we started talking about who we were. Three days later, I had followed her across the entire city: chasing her step goal, revisiting corners of San Francisco she knew by feel and memory, learning to notice what she noticed. The engraved street names pressed into sidewalks. The rhythm of her cane. The specific way a crowd sounds when it parts and closes around you. In a world where everyone is face-down in a phone, Amanda moved through the city with more presence than almost anyone I've ever filmed. Somewhere in those three days, I realized a video wasn't enough. So I started building. First, a braille keyboard game so people could sit with the learning curve she lives inside every day. Then an eye-tracking simulation: what does Amanda's vision actually approximate? What does it feel like to navigate a parade, a coffee shop, a city, with that? And finally, her gallery. Amanda wants to share the footage from her Meta glasses, but the complexity of social media editing puts that far out of reach. So I built her a pipeline: she texts her favorite clips to a contact, a Telegram bot catches them, and they publish automatically to her site. This is just the start. I'm now trying to connect her directly with Meta, to give her a platform to tell them, from lived experience, what their glasses get right and what they still owe people like her. Amanda's story taught me that empathy isn't a feeling. It's a practice. It's what you notice when you finally put the phone down.

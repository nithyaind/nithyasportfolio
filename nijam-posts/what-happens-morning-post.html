What Happens the Morning After Social Media?

By Nithya Sunkara Indlamuri August 2026 8 min read

A very real fear of mine is to be called "performative," more like caught being performative since there is no word more accurate. The clothes I wear, the things I say, the coffee I buy, the car windows I roll down, the nails I pay too much money for, coming to Singapore for exchange—everything is part of the Nithya act, an act about portraying who I want to be seen as. "Fake it 'til you make it" or whatever. What this class made me do is sit with the part I had been avoiding: I don't think I am the exception. I think I am the rule. And I think the rule is quietly making us all insane. Why else do I step into the role of an award-winning actress to pretend to get a phone call to leave rooms I don't want to be in?

So let me ask you, Google Docs and whoever reads this, something that I have been asking my exchanger peers and too scared to answer out loud.

Would you go to Paris if you couldn't take a picture with the Eiffel Tower and post it for your friends to see? Would you jump out of an airplane if you couldn't gloat about it for months as the likes and "Wow!" comments trickle in? Would you have gone to northern Vietnam if you hadn't seen hundreds of pictures of people posing at the same viewpoint, wearing Arc'teryx jackets where the blue looks bluer and the green looks greener thanks to Lightroom presets? So when you get there and realize it is not beautiful enough, you too throw a filter on it, and convince the next person to go learn Photoshop in the mountains.

Do we do anything without the social reward or the need to impress anymore?

I did the Ha Giang Loop a few weeks ago. Days before the trip, the algorithm had already told me what the experience would look like. It told me which viewpoint I would stop at, which local child to photograph, which jacket to wear. And I listened, because that is what participation looks like now. Jenkins calls this kind of collective sharing a revolution: consumers who "actively shape media flows" so that culture becomes "far more participatory" (Jenkins, 2006, p. 135). He is so excited about the spreading, the remixing, the co-creation. What he doesn't discuss is that the thing being spread is increasingly just one image of the same mountain, filtered thirty different ways, by forty different people who all found it on the same reel, all trying to out-authentic each other in the same Arc'teryx jacket.

In this effort to be authentic, these local kids have become paid actors in posts and vlogs giving many of their parents an incentive not to send them to school. The tourists on the other hand are stuck in this cycle of either spamming pictures with the locals or avoiding them so as to not support children being kept from school. However, isn't the most obvious way to be authentic to a culture to talk to people and more importantly, to listen? Why do I believe the narrative the American influencer sold to me online instead of actually asking the mothers of the kids myself? What are we learning from each other online if all of us learn the same mistakes from one another? Is every post a singular frame of limits and constraints? An instruction to interpret?

This is not collective intelligence. This is collective hallucination.

Jenkins borrows from Pierre Lévy the idea that in a knowledge community, "no one knows everything, everyone knows something, all knowledge resides in humanity" (Lévy, as cited in Jenkins, 2006, p. 139). It sounds beautiful and democratic, but the people who are consistently, relentlessly putting media into the world—who are those people? They are people chasing social reward, financial reward, algorithmic reward. The Ha Giang Loop content creator is working for clout. The travel blogger ranking "most authentic experiences in Vietnam" is working for affiliate links. The exchange student posting her bucket list is working for the version of herself she wants other people to believe in. Jenkins's patchwork collective intelligence (Jenkins, 2006, p. 140) doesn't account for the fact that the patches are all being cut by the same machine, for the same incentive structure, handed to us by platforms that, as Fuchs bluntly puts it, exploit "the users' activities and the data they generate."

And what terrifies me most is that we know this and keep going.

Who owns the desire?

When this realization came to me in one of our earlier lectures, I started jotting down a bucket list: farming, scuba diving, Ha Giang Loop, make a film, Songkran. I told myself these were mine, pulled from memory, pre-algorithmic desires and not from the exchanger handbook that we all unanimously adopt. But were they my own ideas? When I try to trace any want back to its origin, I hit the same wall. A feed. A post. A futuristic vision of being able to tell my friends and family that I did it. A movie. Someone else's highlight reel that became my ambition. Fuchs writes that Jenkins ignores "questions about the ownership of platforms, collective decision-making, profit, class and the distribution of material benefits." I want to push that further: Jenkins also ignores the question of who owns the desire. Who decided what a life worth living looks like? Because somewhere between my childhood and my Changi arrival, someone authored the Exchanger script and it wasn't me. Most passport stamps, wins. And I committed to it like it was mine. Jenkins referenced Mark Dery's dose of caution that "the media industries will find alternative means of marginalizing and disenfranchising citizen participation" (Dery, as cited in Jenkins, 2006, p. 150). What if this is not a future worry but a current system we can't even distinguish from normal anymore? Because, truly, can any corporate company in the media exist purely for participation even in the wake of capitalism?

Then, the question is not whether social media has made us performative. The question is whether, without it, we would do a fraction of the things we say we love.

The morning after

I have been sitting with this since I rewatched old episodes of Supergirl recently, specifically the ones where superheroes visit present-day Earth from the future and reference social media the way we reference the telegraph: as a thing that existed, briefly, and then ended. It made me wonder if that day would come. Not with a bang but maybe with a data scandal too large to ignore, or a government that bans everything, or simply a generation that gets so exhausted by the politics and performance that it collectively, quietly, stops. What happens the morning after social media?

Do we still go to Paris? Do we still jump off the cliff? Do we go to the mountain and stand there, unfiltered, with no one watching, and find out whether we actually think it's beautiful?

I don't know. That is the most honest thing I have written in this essay. I do not know who I am without an audience and I am not sure I have ever been given the chance to find out. An example is uncovered in writing this essay where I haven't censored myself of em dashes for the first time since the conception of widespread LLM usage, a regular performance for my professors and peers but inauthentic to me as a writer. I don't know if I actually love the mountains, because when my grandmother looks at my pictures of Vietnam's mountains, she laughs that it looks like her town. I argue but secretly agree. Why am I trying to sell opinions that I don't know if I even believe but have scrolled past to my Instagram-free grandmother? Thus, rather than a digital detox for the aesthetic, documented in a carousel post upon return, I wonder if we can all challenge ourselves to do something with no proof. Letting an experience be so entirely yours that it dies with you, unseen, unliked, unspread. Taking a break from influencing everyone around us to really notice what is influencing us.

Thank you to my professor Dr. Shobha Avadhani. I remember telling my friends and family at home after my first week of classes that one day I want to be as cool and badass as you are. Many of the ideas, thoughts, and questions I have today are seeds you panted. I am super grateful to you always.

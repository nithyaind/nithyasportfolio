The Story of SSO

By Nithya Sunkara Indlamuri August 2026 6 min read

My second summer at Fidelity Investments began with the illusion of familiarity: the same badge with the same awkward headshot, the same parking habits, the same lunch orders, the same anticipation for curly fries and complimentary Sun Chips. But what I actually learned this summer looked nothing like last year.

I'm a rising senior at UNC Chapel Hill studying Computer Science and Media & Journalism, and this summer I worked out of Fidelity's Durham office as a Software Engineer intern on Workplace Investing's Single Sign-On (SSO) team. Last summer, I interned with Fidelity's Student Debt team on the Quality Engineering side. During a coffee chat back then, a product leader, Akhil Nigam, told me something that's stuck with me since:

"It's not about coming up with the perfect solution, but about identifying a great problem."

At the time, that felt relevant mostly to my work as a QE, and to someone who's always chasing the perfect, world-changing idea. But it's evolved into something bigger for me since.

As a journalist, I publish articles, produce films, and meet a lot of people hoping to raise awareness about something. Usually, taking action on it becomes someone else's responsibility. As a journalist, I listen and report. I identify problems. With tech, I get to design and build some form of the solution. That's why I love what I get to do.

My Fidtern Project

This summer, I had the chance to work with Workplace Investing's Customer Protection and SSO team on the developer side, under my manager, Alex Leach. Our team sits at a unique intersection: on one side are the developers building and improving the technology, and on the other are the analysts working directly with clients to configure, onboard, and support SSO connections. That combination gives the team a rare perspective — understanding both how the product works and how people actually use it.

Alongside my co-intern, Nicholas Houghton (pronounced "Ho-tin," like "waitin'"), I worked on SSO Admin — the application our analysts use to onboard and manage clients and their SSO and password-based connections. As the platform grows, the goal is to make more of these processes accessible to teams who aren't already experts in the system, which means the product needs to be intuitive, discoverable, and resistant to human error.

Our project focused on a challenge that was simple to describe and surprisingly impactful to fix. SSO currently serves 900+ clients across inbound and outbound connections, and each client has a unique ID — a long string of numbers that doesn't tell much of a story on its own. Most of the search and dashboard views in SSO Admin were client-ID-only. Our feature lets analysts see the corresponding client name right alongside the ID, making it much faster to verify configurations and validate their work — and we paired it with a search function with clear error handling, so analysts get real feedback when an ID is invalid or already exists. We shipped this across four pages. In other words: we helped turn a collection of numbers into meaningful context. What started as an API that had never actually been used evolved into a fully integrated, production-ready feature.

Building it gave me exposure to the full development lifecycle. I built the frontend in Angular and TypeScript, worked with REST clients and Java/Spring Boot on the backend, tested APIs in Bruno, tracked the work in Jira, and managed pull requests through GitHub — all inside a large, genuinely enterprise-scale codebase. I also got to shadow how a feature like this actually gets deployed to production, which is a very different thing than reading about it.

I also came away with a much better sense of what security-minded development actually looks like, thanks to Secure Code Warrior training and to what I'd already picked up last summer as a QE reviewing PRs. That foundation mattered even more this summer, since I got to experiment with GitHub Copilot throughout the development process. I used it to help explain the large codebase early on, which cut my onboarding time significantly and let me start contributing faster. Once I moved into actual development, I leaned on it at every stage — from writing to debugging — mostly so I could figure out what it was actually good at and where it fell short. That was, at the same time, a test of my own ability to review code. With enough practice, I'm hoping bad code will eventually start to stick out to me the way an em dash does.

The Conversations

As valuable as the technical skills were, they weren't the biggest takeaway. What I learned most this summer came from the conversations.

It's a genuine privilege to work in the security space at a moment like this one in tech. Between increasingly capable AI systems like Claude Mythos, shifting policy, powerful open-source models, and threats that evolve faster than most teams can plan for, every organization is being pushed to move quickly — and Fidelity carries the added weight of maintaining client trust on top of that. Between the people around me, programs like TechX, questions like "Fast but How Far," coffee chats, and LEAP panels, I sometimes felt like I had a seat in the mission control room of a rocket launch.

Watching the SSO team respond to real security threats — migrating to better systems like Compass, and asking hard questions about their own existing processes and standards — was genuinely insightful. And just because we're technically gearing up to defend against what AI can do doesn't mean we're against it: the inventive ways my colleagues were already using it to work better and faster kept sparking new ideas about where else it could be applied.

Beyond getting to ship a production-ready feature for my team, I came away actually understanding the "hows" and "whys" behind the company's products, its operating model, and the decisions behind both — which, it turns out, was the real lesson underneath all of it:

Understand the "why" behind the product. The story.

If it isn't obvious by now, I love stories. So this felt like the right way to tell this one.

Thank you to Alex, for being an incredibly thoughtful and intentional manager and mentor. To Nick — I loved getting to work with you this summer. To Govinda, Mansi, Hari, Jithender, and Adam, thank you for your patience, your guidance, and for answering what felt like 562,940 questions from me. Your command of the product — both the business side and the technology — and your initiative were genuinely inspiring, and I'm grateful to the whole SSO team. To my mentor, David Schneir, who shares my love of stories (his are definitely the cooler ones) — thank you for going above and beyond, and for all the conversations, past and still to come.

Shoutout to my intern buddies for the snack alerts and the laughs, the University Talent team and Kent for the support and the fun events, and everyone else I got to talk to this summer. I'll miss Hari's positivity — even when his Zoom thumbs-ups were accidental — Anika's word games at stand-up, and Cam's "okay byeee" at the end of every meeting. Thank you for another awesome summer.
